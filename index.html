This isn't about rules.
It's about being aware of the atmosphere.
Small details can make a big difference in how you're perceived.

These are cultural etiquette norms, not official laws — and not everyone in Japan follows them identically. They're here simply to help you avoid unintentionally making others uncomfortable.

🚫 Strictly Not Acceptable

Strongly discouraged — can cause trouble

Wearing shoes indoors

→ Homes, temples, traditional restaurants, and ryokan inns. Always check the entrance and follow the local custom.

Not lining up properly

→ Trains, elevators, popular restaurants, and even small shops. Japan runs on orderly queues.

Not separating trash or recycling properly

→ Public trash bins can be rare, but sorting rules are strict where bins are provided. Follow the instructions on the bin.

⚠️ Not Preferred

Culturally uncomfortable, but generally tolerated

Strong perfume / heavy fragrance

→ Subtlety is valued in Japan, especially in enclosed spaces such as trains.

Speaking loudly in public

→ On trains, in cafés, and on the street. People generally keep their voices low.

Talking on the phone on trains or in restaurants

→ On trains, phones are normally kept on silent mode and phone calls are generally avoided. In restaurants, be mindful of the people around you.

Public displays of affection (PDA)

→ Holding hands is generally fine. More intimate displays can attract attention.

Revealing clothing / excessive exposure

→ Urban Japan is fairly relaxed, but modest clothing is still the cultural norm in many situations.

Native Tip — This Is the Key

If you're unsure, simply observe the people around you.

Japan often works more on:

"Don't disturb others."

than on:

"Follow every rule."

Master this simple idea, and you'll understand Japanese culture much more easily.

3 Signs You Should Know in Japan

In Japanese cities, some important messages are communicated without words. Knowing these signs can help you notice when someone may need extra care or consideration.

📷https://pub-7806a0647a9943448e15f9e00f8ed59e.r2.dev/images/travel-tips/caution1.jpg 1

The Help Mark

Invisible Disabilities

The Help Mark is a red tag featuring a white cross and heart. It's used by people who may need assistance even though their disabilities or medical conditions aren't immediately visible — this can include internal medical conditions, prosthetic limbs, or conditions such as panic disorders.

WHAT TO DO: If you see someone wearing this mark on a train or in a public place, be considerate. If appropriate, offer your seat or give them extra space.
📷https://pub-7806a0647a9943448e15f9e00f8ed59e.r2.dev/images/travel-tips/caution2.jpg 2

The Maternity Mark

Supporting Expectant Mothers

The Maternity Mark is a pink symbol used by pregnant women. It's especially important during the early stages of pregnancy, when pregnancy may not yet be physically obvious.

WHAT TO DO: If you notice this mark on crowded public transport or in busy areas, be mindful of the person wearing it. Offer your seat if possible, especially when seating is limited.
📷https://pub-7806a0647a9943448e15f9e00f8ed59e.r2.dev/images/travel-tips/caution3.jpg 3

Yellow Braille Blocks

Keep the Path Clear

The yellow textured lines found on sidewalks and station platforms are called Tenji Blocks. They're an important navigation system for people with visual impairments.

WHAT TO DO: Never stand on these blocks to take photos, wait, or leave luggage. Keep them completely clear so people who rely on them can move safely and independently.
Small Signs. Big Difference.

Understanding these simple signs can help you be a more considerate traveler in Japan.

A little awareness goes a long way.

⛩️ Shrine or Temple?

Which one would you visit for a wedding? Which one would you visit for a funeral?

Can You Guess?

💍 Wedding → ?

⚱️ Funeral → ?

The Answer

💍 Weddings → Shinto shrines

⚱️ Funerals → Buddhist temples

Temples usually have a cemetery nearby because of their connection with ancestors, memorials, and funerals.

This simple difference explains a lot about how shrines and temples are used.

🙏 What Do You Pray For?

This is the real difference.

⛩️ Shrines — Shinto

You pray for life events and new beginnings:

  • Success
  • Love
  • Health
  • New beginnings
  • Good fortune

🏯 Temples — Buddhism

You pray for reflection, remembrance, and the afterlife:

  • Ancestors
  • Peace
  • Remembrance
  • Reflection

⛩️ Shrines — Shinto

For life, beginnings, and celebration

  • Weddings
  • New Year visits
  • Prayers for success and good fortune

Connected to nature and purity

  • Torii gates mark the entrance
  • Shrine grounds are often open and connected with nature
  • Visitors usually come to pray and continue on rather than spending a long time inside

Japanese Shrine Worship Process

  • Two Bows: Bow deeply twice.
  • Two Claps: Clap your hands twice at chest height.
  • Prayer / Wish: Keep hands together and offer a silent prayer or wish.
  • One Bow: Bow deeply once more to conclude.

A common pattern, not a strict universal rule.

🏯 Temples — Buddhism

For death, reflection, and remembrance

  • Funerals
  • Memorial services
  • Family graves
  • Remembering ancestors

Places for contemplation

  • Incense
  • Buddhist statues
  • Quiet gardens and walking areas

A quieter spiritual experience

Visitors may spend more time exploring the temple grounds, buildings, gardens, and surrounding spaces.

👕 What Should You Wear?

⛩️ Shrines

Generally relaxed. Casual clothing is usually fine, while respectful behavior is expected.

🏯 Temples

A more modest style is preferred. Best to avoid:

  • Very revealing clothing
  • Excessively short clothing
  • Tank tops in more traditional or sacred settings
  • Excessive exposure

These aren't strict universal dress codes — expectations can vary depending on the temple, shrine, location, and occasion.

👀 How Can You Tell Instantly?

⛩️ Shrines

Look for:

  • Torii gates
  • Open grounds
  • Nature
  • A brighter, more open atmosphere

🏯 Temples

Look for:

  • Large traditional buildings
  • Buddhist statues
  • Incense
  • Gardens
  • A quieter atmosphere
Native Tip

Don't Overthink Religion.

People naturally visit both shrines and temples at different moments in life.

A shrine for one occasion. A temple for another.

That balance is part of the culture.

🥢 Chopstick Etiquette

Small gestures make a big difference.

How Good Are Your Chopstick Manners?

Some chopstick habits are more than just "bad manners" — they can have cultural or historical meanings.

Don't worry about being perfect.

Knowing the basics is already a big step.

Before You Start

Having trouble with chopsticks?

Just ask for a fork or spoon. Many restaurants have them available — simply ask the staff. Don't feel embarrassed if chopsticks are difficult.

🚫 Strictly Not Acceptable

Avoid these

🍚🥢 Never stick chopsticks upright in rice Tsukitate-bashi

Sticking chopsticks vertically into a bowl of rice resembles a Buddhist funeral ritual. Never do this at the dining table.

🥢→🥢 Never pass food chopstick to chopstick Hiroi-bashi

Passing food directly from one pair of chopsticks to another is also associated with funeral rituals. Use a plate or serving utensil instead.

🥢🍱 Don't stab food with chopsticks Sashi-bashi

Using chopsticks like a fork to spear food is considered rude. Pick food up gently instead.

⚠️ Not Preferred

Try to avoid these

Licking your chopsticks Neburi-bashi

Licking food from your chopsticks is generally considered unhygienic and childish.

Letting food or sauce drip Namida-bashi

Carrying food while sauce or liquid drips from it can look careless.

Pointing with chopsticks Yubisashi-bashi

Using chopsticks to point at people or objects can feel aggressive or impolite.

Moving dishes with chopsticks Yose-bashi

Don't use your chopsticks to pull or push dishes toward you. Use your hands instead.

Hovering while choosing food Mayoi-bashi

Moving your chopsticks back and forth over several dishes while deciding what to take can look indecisive. Choose first, then pick up your food.

👍 How to Use Chopsticks Properly

  • ✓ Pick up food gently — don't stab it.
  • ✓ Rest your chopsticks when you're not using them.
  • ✓ Keep chopstick movements quiet and controlled.
  • ✓ When sharing food, use serving utensils when available.
  • ✓ If serving utensils aren't provided, use the opposite end of your chopsticks when appropriate.

Even many people in Japan don't perfectly follow every one of these points. Knowing them just helps you avoid obvious mistakes and come across as a considerate diner.

Native Tip

It's Not About Being Perfect.

You don't need to master every chopstick rule.

But clean, quiet, and considerate movements make a great impression.

Small gestures show respect.

💴 Tipping in Japan

No tip needed. That's normal in Japan.

Here's the surprising part...

In Japan, tipping is generally not expected. Restaurants, hotels, taxis, cafés, and most everyday services do not require a tip. You don't need to calculate 15%, 20%, or 25%.

The price you see is normally the price you pay.

Do You Need to Tip?

Which situations might involve a personal thank-you? Tap each to find out.

🍱 Restaurants

No tip needed — ever.

From casual restaurants to high-end dining, you do not need to leave a tip. If you accidentally leave money behind, staff may even think you forgot it.

Why? Because in Japan, great service isn't considered "extra" — it's part of the service itself.

🏨 Traditional Luxury Ryokan

A rare exception: Kokorozuke

At some high-end traditional ryokan, a personal thank-you known as Kokorozuke (お心付け) may be given in special circumstances. It's not a standard service charge or required tip — it's a quiet gesture of appreciation, traditionally given discreetly.

How?

If giving cash, place it in a small envelope rather than handing over loose bills. If you don't have an envelope, hand it discreetly rather than displaying the cash openly.

This is optional and uncommon, not expected at every luxury ryokan — a gesture of appreciation, not payment for service.

🧑‍🏫 Private Guides & Personal Services

Another situation where a personal thank-you may be appropriate

For a private guide or highly personalized service, some travelers may choose to give a small cash gift as a personal expression of thanks — more naturally understood as "Thank you for taking such good care of us" rather than "Here is your tip."

This isn't mandatory or expected — a sincere verbal thank-you is completely appropriate.

🚫 When NOT to Tip

  • ❌ Restaurants
  • ❌ Cafés
  • ❌ Taxis
  • ❌ Hotels
  • ❌ Convenience stores
  • ❌ Ordinary tours and activities
  • ❌ Everyday services

In normal situations, just pay the bill and say thank you.

The idea isn't "pay more for kindness." It's closer to "good service is already part of what you receive."

💡 Native Insight

The best hospitality can be almost invisible.

Hospitality in Japan is often quiet, precise, considerate, unobtrusive. You may not even notice how much effort went into the service — that's one reason tipping never became a normal part of everyday life here.

So, What Should You Do?

Don't worry about tipping.

Enjoy the service. Pay the bill.

Say "Arigato gozaimasu."

That's perfectly fine.
